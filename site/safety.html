Safety & harm reduction

Most of the compounds documented on this site are not approved for human use, and several are prescription-only medicines. This page exists because people use them anyway, and the information gap makes that more dangerous than it needs to be.

Get urgent medical care for any of these Chest pain or pressure. Difficulty breathing. Swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat. A hot, spreading red area around an injection site, especially with fever or rigors. Severe persistent vomiting or signs of dehydration. Sudden vision change. Fainting. Severe abdominal pain radiating to the back. None of these are questions for a forum, and none improve with waiting.

Before anything else

Three questions decide most of the risk, and none of them are about the compound:

  • Is there a diagnosis? Fatigue, poor sleep, low libido, joint pain and stubborn weight all have investigable causes. Reaching for an unapproved peptide before basic bloodwork means treating a symptom whose origin you have not established, and potentially masking something that needed attention.
  • Does a clinician know? Many people assume disclosure is pointless because their doctor will disapprove. The practical cost of non-disclosure is real: unexplained lab changes get investigated expensively, and interactions go unnoticed. Interactions with GLP-1 agonists and anaesthesia are now a live pre-operative issue specifically because people did not mention them.
  • What would make you stop? Decide the stopping criteria before you start, in writing. People who have not pre-committed reliably rationalise adverse effects as adjustment periods.

Injection technique

Injection-site infection is the most common serious adverse event reported in community peptide use, and it is almost entirely preventable.

  • Hands first. Wash thoroughly before touching anything. This is the step most often skipped and the one that matters most.
  • Swab the vial stopper with 70% isopropyl alcohol every single time, including on a vial you opened five minutes ago, and let it dry. Wet alcohol carried into tissue stings and does not disinfect.
  • Swab the site and let it air dry rather than fanning or blowing on it.
  • One needle, one use. Needles blunt measurably on first insertion; reuse increases tissue trauma and contamination risk. Never share, under any circumstance.
  • Rotate sites across abdomen and thigh quadrants. Repeated injection into one spot produces lipohypertrophy, which then alters absorption unpredictably — a genuine and under-appreciated cause of "it stopped working".
  • Sharps go in a proper container, not a household bin. Most pharmacies and many councils take them.
  • Air bubbles in a subcutaneous injection are not dangerous, but they displace volume — a bubble means you injected less than you drew.

Storage & degradation

Peptides are fragile in ways small molecules are not. Degradation is usually invisible, so handling discipline substitutes for the test you cannot run.

Storage guidance for lyophilised and reconstituted peptides
StateTypical handlingFailure mode
Lyophilised, sealed−20 °C for long-term, refrigerated for months, light-protectedMoisture ingress; the powder cakes or discolours
Reconstituted2–8 °C, upright, light-protectedHydrolysis and oxidation over weeks; cloudiness or particulates
Freeze–thawAvoid entirely once in solutionAggregation — often irreversible and not always visible
In transitAssume a warm chain unless proven otherwiseCumulative thermal exposure you never see

Bacteriostatic water is not sterile water. Bacteriostatic water contains 0.9% benzyl alcohol, which inhibits microbial growth and makes multi-dose use over weeks reasonable. Sterile water contains no preservative: once the stopper is pierced, the contents should be treated as single-use. Benzyl alcohol is also contraindicated in neonates and in some people with sensitivities, and it can affect the stability of certain peptides.

Reconstitute gently. Aim the diluent stream at the vial wall and let it run down onto the powder, then swirl — do not shake. Shaking creates an air–liquid interface that denatures peptides, and foam is a visible sign it has happened.

Inspect before every dose. Cloudiness, particulates, colour change, or a powder that has visibly shifted appearance means discard. There is no rescuing a compromised vial and no way to assess it by eye beyond that.

Change one thing at a time

The single most common methodological failure in community reporting is starting three compounds in the same week alongside a new training block and a diet change, then attributing the result to whichever compound was most expensive.

  • Establish a baseline for at least two weeks before changing anything: relevant bloodwork, weight, sleep, a training metric, whatever your endpoint actually is.
  • Introduce one variable. Hold it long enough to see an effect — for a GH-axis compound that is weeks, not days.
  • Record the stop reason when you stop. This is the field that makes a log useful to anybody else, and the one almost everyone omits.
  • Expect placebo. It is large for subjective endpoints like energy, mood, libido and pain, which is precisely the territory most of these compounds are marketed on.

Interactions worth knowing

  • GLP-1 agonists and surgery. Delayed gastric emptying raises aspiration risk under anaesthesia. Anaesthesia guidance now addresses this directly — tell your surgical team, well before the day.
  • GLP-1 agonists and oral medication. Absorption of oral drugs, including oral contraceptives, can change after dose escalation.
  • GH-axis compounds and glucose. Growth hormone stimulation opposes insulin. If you monitor glucose or are managing diabetes, this is a measurable interaction, not a theoretical one.
  • Immunomodulators and immunotherapy. Thymosin α1, KPV and VIP all modulate immune signalling. Combining them with checkpoint inhibitors or immunosuppressants is unstudied and potentially significant.
  • Angiogenic compounds and malignancy. BPC-157 and TB-500 are proposed to promote angiogenesis. Any personal history of cancer makes this a conversation with an oncologist, not a self-directed decision.
  • Melanocortin agonists and blood pressure. Bremelanotide transiently raises blood pressure with every dose; melanotan II is non-selective and less predictable still.

If you compete

Most compounds in this library are prohibited by WADA at all times, including BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, all GH secretagogues and all GHRH analogues. A therapeutic use exemption is not available for most of them. "Research peptide" is not a defence, and neither is a contaminated-supplement claim without documentation. Check the current Prohibited List directly rather than trusting any vendor's assertion.

Legal status

Legality varies enormously and changes. In the US, most of these are unapproved drugs — sale for human consumption is illegal, which is exactly why vendors label them "research use only", and several were placed on the FDA's Category 2 bulk-substances list in 2023. In the UK, Australia and much of the EU, importation of prescription-only medicines without a prescription is a customs and regulatory matter. Nothing on this site is legal advice, and none of it should be read as encouragement to obtain anything.
