Sourcing & verification

We do not name vendors, rank them, or accept anything from them. What we can do is teach the document literacy that makes vendor claims checkable — starting with the certificate almost nobody reads properly.

Why there is no vendor list here Every peptide "vendor ranking" on the internet is affiliate-funded, and the ones that claim otherwise are usually just earlier in the funnel. A ranking is worth exactly as much as its incentive structure, so we publish none. If a page on this site ever recommends a seller, assume the site has been compromised and tell us.

Reading a certificate of analysis

A CoA is a lab report for one specific lot. It is not a quality badge and it is not transferable. Four things determine whether it means anything:

  • Does the lot number on the document match the vial in your hand?

    The most common failure is not a forged certificate — it is a real certificate for a different lot, reused across an entire product line. If the vial has no lot number, the certificate is decorative.

  • Is there a mass spectrometry trace, and does the mass match?

    This is the identity check. HPLC tells you how much of the sample is one thing; mass spec tells you what that thing is. A certificate with purity but no MS proves the vial contains 99% of something. Compare the observed mass against the reference value in our library — this is exactly what catches TB-500 fragment sold as full-length thymosin β4.

  • Is the HPLC chromatogram shown, or just a number?

    A stated "99.2%" with no trace is an assertion. The chromatogram shows the peak shape, the baseline and the impurity peaks that the headline number averages away. Co-eluting impurities are invisible in the summary figure and visible in the trace.

  • Who ran it, and did the vendor choose them?

    An in-house certificate has an obvious conflict. An independent lab is better, but a vendor-commissioned independent test still lets the vendor choose which lot to submit and whether to publish the result.

What a CoA is silent about

Even a genuine, matched, mass-spec-backed certificate leaves large gaps. These are the ones that matter:

Assays commonly absent from peptide certificates of analysis
Not usually testedWhy it matters
Bacterial endotoxin (LAL)Endotoxin survives sterilisation and causes fever and inflammatory reactions. It is the single most relevant missing assay for anything injected, and it is almost never on a research-peptide CoA.
SterilityPurity is a chemistry measurement. It says nothing about microbial contamination of the finished vial.
Residual solventsSynthesis and cleavage use TFA, DMF, piperidine and others. Residual trifluoroacetate is routinely present in peptides purified by reverse-phase HPLC and is rarely quantified.
Peptide content vs. net weightA "5 mg" vial usually weighs 5 mg gross — including counterion, water and residual salts. Actual peptide content can be meaningfully lower, and amino acid analysis is the assay that would tell you.
Heavy metalsRelevant for metal-complexed peptides and for anything synthesised without controlled inputs.
Stability over shelf lifeA CoA is a snapshot at manufacture. It tells you nothing about what a warm shipping container did afterwards.

Third-party testing

Buyer-commissioned independent testing is the strongest signal available to the community, and it is still limited. What it does well: identity confirmation by mass, purity by HPLC, and detection of gross substitution or under-filling. What it cannot do: certify sterility of a vial that has already been opened for sampling, retrospectively assess the storage history, or say anything about a different lot from the same source.

Two practical points. First, sampling destroys the vial's sterility, so the tested unit should be treated as consumed. Second, a single passing result generalises poorly — batch-to-batch variation is the norm in this market, not the exception, and a lot that tested clean six months ago tells you very little about the one shipped today.

Signals worth weighing

  • Price far below the market. Synthesis cost has a floor. A price well beneath everyone else's usually means under-filled vials, not efficiency.
  • Clinical claims in the marketing. A seller who writes "supports recovery and vitality" about a Tier 4 compound is telling you how carefully they read the literature.
  • "99% purity" as a site-wide banner. Purity is a per-lot measurement. As a blanket marketing claim across a catalogue, it is meaningless by construction.
  • No lot numbers on vials. Nothing downstream can be verified without them.
  • Blends sold as single products. A "BPC-157/TB-500 blend" cannot be dose-adjusted per component, and its CoA typically characterises neither properly.
  • Pressure and scarcity mechanics. Countdown timers and stock warnings on a product marketed for research use are aimed at consumers, and the framing tells you who the actual customer is.

On "research use only"

This phrase is a legal position, not a description of the customer. Sellers use it because marketing an unapproved drug for human consumption is illegal in most jurisdictions; the label shifts liability while the product page, dosing chart and syringe upsell address someone who plainly intends to inject it. Being clear-eyed about that is part of assessing risk honestly: the label is not a safety claim, and it is not a quality standard. It means no regulator has assessed what is in the vial.
